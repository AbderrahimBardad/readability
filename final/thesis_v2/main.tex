\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Arabic readability assessment estimates how difficult a text is for a target reader. Readability
levels form an ordered scale, yet most systems treat them as unrelated classes and lose the fact
that an error of one level is smaller than an error of ten. Before 2025, Arabic readability work
relied on formulas adapted from English, on classifiers trained on coarse school or learner
scales, and on lexicons and corpora that label words or whole texts; this thesis reviews that
work and what it could not support, and builds on the BAREC corpus, which annotates 69,441 Arabic
sentences on 19 ordered levels.

The thesis compares ordinal regression heads and loss functions for fine-grained Arabic
readability on BAREC. The pipeline stays the same in every experiment: the corpus and its
official splits, the morphologically segmented D3Tok input, an AraBERTv2 encoder, and the
training protocol of the BAREC benchmark, including its library defaults. Only the prediction
head and the loss function change. Nine configurations form the comparable group: cross-entropy,
regression with mean squared error, ordinal log loss, an Earth Mover's Distance loss, four soft
label settings and a CORAL ordinal head. A two-phase schedule, which warms up with mean squared
error and then optimises a differentiable Soft-QWK objective, is reported separately. Three
losses that the benchmark does not evaluate are then added from readability research in other
languages: CORN, which a BAREC system names as future work, and the pairwise margin ranking and
pairwise logistic losses of multilingual and English, French and Spanish readability models.
Three ablations test the learning rate of the CORAL head, the decision threshold and the
checkpoint selection rule. A second track freezes the backbone and trains linear heads on cached
features over five seeds; it meets the research topic's requirement of being CPU-friendly and
gives variance estimates.

The pipeline reproduces the published benchmark: seven of its eight losses land within 1.5 points
of quadratic weighted kappa (QWK), and the checkpoint selection rule explains the eighth. Ordinal
aware objectives beat the ordinal-blind baseline by up to three QWK points on the development set
and four on the test set. Four objectives share the lead: the CORAL head (\testQwkCoral\ test
QWK), the two-phase Soft-QWK schedule (\testQwkSoftqwk), ordinal log loss (\testQwkOLL) and plain
regression (\testQwkMSE); their differences are a few tenths of a point on a single seed, so the
thesis does not name one best loss. When the errors are split by distance, the leaders turn out to
gain their QWK by removing far misses and not by lowering the average error. Of the added losses,
the margin ranking loss joins the leading group on the test set (\nlTestQwkRank), the pairwise
logistic loss makes the fewest errors of five levels or more of any run (\nlTestFarFivePlr\ per
cent on test), and CORN, which removes CORAL's shared projection, trades \nlAbsDeltaTestQwkCorn\
QWK points for \nlAbsDeltaTestAccCorn\ points of exact accuracy and the lowest mean distance of the
study. Two results are about the protocol and not the loss. The cut-points of a CORAL head move
less than \cutMoveFineCoral\ under the benchmark learning rate, so their initialisation decides
where they end up, and this thesis initialises them from the cumulative label frequencies of the
training set. The checkpoint selection rule on its own is worth up to \qwkselTestGainMax\ test QWK
points, and is worth most to the methods whose loss is least aligned with the metric.

{\textbf{Keywords:}} Arabic readability assessment, BAREC, AraBERTv2, D3Tok, ordinal regression,
CORAL, CORN, pairwise ranking, Soft-QWK, quadratic weighted kappa, error distance.
