\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}  
Arabic readability assessment estimates how difficult a text is for a target reader. Readability levels form an ordered scale, yet most systems treat them as unrelated classes and lose the fact that an error of one level is smaller than an error of ten.

This thesis compares ordinal regression heads and loss functions for fine grained Arabic readability assessment on the BAREC corpus, which annotates 69,441 Arabic sentences on 19 ordered levels. The pipeline stays the same in every experiment: the corpus and its official splits, the morphologically segmented D3Tok input, an AraBERTv2 encoder, and the training protocol of the BAREC benchmark, including its library defaults. Only the prediction head and the loss function change. Nine configurations form the comparable group: cross entropy, regression with mean squared error, ordinal log loss, an Earth Mover's Distance loss, four soft label settings and a CORAL ordinal head. A schedule in two phases, which warms up with mean squared error and then optimises a differentiable Soft QWK objective, is reported separately. Three ablations test the learning rate of the CORAL head, the decision threshold and the checkpoint selection rule. A second track freezes the backbone and trains linear heads on cached features over five seeds. It meets the research topic's requirement of being CPU friendly and gives variance estimates.

The pipeline reproduces the published benchmark: seven of its eight losses land within 1.5 points of quadratic weighted kappa (QWK), and the checkpoint selection rule explains the eighth. Ordinal aware objectives beat the ordinal blind baseline by up to three QWK points on the development set and four on the test set. Four objectives share the lead: the CORAL head(\testQwkCoral\ test QWK), the two-phase Soft-QWK schedule (\testQwkSoftqwk), ordinal log loss (\testQwkOLL) and plain regression (\testQwkMSE); their differences are a few tenths of a point on a single seed, so the thesis does not name one best loss. When the errors are split by distance, the leaders turn out to gain their QWK by removing far misses and not by lowering the average error. CORAL has the fewest errors of three levels or more(\testFarThreeCoral\ per cent) while the smallest mean distance and the highest exact accuracy go to a soft label setting that sits two QWK points behind. Two results are about the protocol and not the loss. The cut points of a CORAL head move less than\cutMoveFineCoral\under the benchmark learning rate, so in practice their initialisation decides where they end up. For that reason this thesis initialises them from the cumulative label frequencies of the training set. The checkpoint selection rule on its own is worth up to \qwkselTestGainMax\ test QWK points, and is worth most to the methods whose loss is least aligned with the metric.

{\textbf{Keywords:}} Arabic readability assessment, BAREC, AraBERTv2, ordinal regression, CORAL, Soft QWK, quadratic weighted kappa, distance aware loss functions, error distance.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background-motivation}

Readability assessment is an important research topic in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and educational technology. It aims to estimate how easily a text can be understood by a particular group of readers, considering both the linguistic characteristics of the text and the abilities of its intended audience \cite{dubay2004principles,vajjala2022trends}. Readability is therefore relevant to understanding the relationship between textual complexity and reader proficiency.

In educational contexts, readability assessment can support the selection of appropriate learning materials, curriculum design, intelligent tutoring systems, and text simplification \cite{vajjala2022trends}. Estimating text difficulty can help educators identify materials that are suitable for learners at different educational levels. It can also support the development of resources adapted to specific reading abilities. Reliable readability assessment is thus an important component of educational applications that seek to improve access to written information.

Automatic readability assessment aims to estimate text difficulty computationally rather than relying exclusively on manual judgments. Traditional methods often use surface-level textual features, such as word length and sentence length, to approximate difficulty. However, text comprehension may also depend on more complex linguistic properties, including vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and semantic relationships. This motivates the investigation of computational methods capable of learning richer representations of text and capturing patterns associated with different levels of readability.

Recent developments in machine learning, deep learning, and transformer-based language models have expanded the possibilities for automatic text analysis. These approaches can learn contextual representations from textual data and use them to predict readability levels. Nevertheless, their effectiveness depends on factors such as the characteristics of the target language, the availability of annotated datasets, and the design of the learning objective.

Arabic readability assessment presents particular linguistic and computational challenges. The rich morphology of Arabic, variation in word forms, syntactic complexity, and lexical ambiguity can complicate the representation and analysis of Arabic texts \cite{nassiri2023approaches}. These characteristics make Arabic readability assessment a relevant setting in which to investigate how language-specific properties and modeling decisions affect the prediction of text difficulty.

A further challenge arises when readability is represented through multiple ordered levels. Unlike nominal categories, ordered readability labels express a progression in difficulty, meaning that the distance between a prediction and its reference level can be relevant. For example, predicting a level adjacent to the correct level represents a smaller ordinal discrepancy than predicting a level far away from it. Conventional multiclass classification does not explicitly encode this distance in its standard cross-entropy objective, motivating the investigation of learning strategies that account for the ordinal structure of the target labels \cite{castagnos2022ordinal}.

Motivated by these considerations, this thesis investigates fine-grained Arabic readability assessment by comparing different prediction heads and loss functions within a consistent experimental framework. The study uses the Balanced Arabic Readability Evaluation Corpus (BAREC) as its benchmark and adopts Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) as its principal evaluation metric. The central aim is to examine how alternative learning strategies handle ordered readability levels and to establish an empirical basis for comparing their behavior under a controlled experimental setup.

The Arabic-specific challenges, the limitations of existing approaches, and the precise research gap addressed by this thesis are discussed in the following sections.

\section{Challenges in Arabic Readability Assessment}
\label{sec:arabic-readability-challenges}

Arabic readability assessment presents several linguistic and computational challenges. The morphological richness of Arabic, its extensive use of clitics, lexical ambiguity, and variation in syntactic structure can affect how texts are represented and analyzed by automatic systems. These challenges become particularly relevant in fine-grained readability assessment, where a model must distinguish between multiple ordered levels of text difficulty.

\subsection{Morphological Complexity and Cliticization}

Arabic morphology is characterized by root-based derivation and the interaction of roots, patterns, and affixes. A single root can give rise to multiple related words and inflected forms, while prefixes and suffixes can encode grammatical or semantic information. Consequently, words with related lexical meanings may exhibit different surface forms, increasing the complexity of tokenization, morphological analysis, and text representation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{morphology.png}
    \caption{Illustration of Arabic morphological derivation from the root ``k-t-b'', showing examples of related word forms.}
    \label{fig:arabic_morphology}
\end{figure}

Arabic also exhibits extensive \emph{cliticization}, whereby elements such as conjunctions, prepositions, definite articles, and pronouns attach to a host word. As a result, a single orthographic word may contain several linguistically meaningful units. For example, the form \textit{wa-al-fid\d{d}a} can be analyzed as the conjunction \textit{wa-} (and), the definite article \textit{al-} (the), and the noun \textit{fid\d{d}a} (silver). The exact segmentation depends on the morphological analysis adopted.

Cliticization has practical implications for Arabic text processing because the number of orthographic words does not necessarily correspond to the number of underlying linguistic units. The treatment of clitics can therefore affect tokenization, morphological representation, and the extraction of text features relevant to readability assessment. The BAREC annotation guidelines discuss linguistic characteristics associated with readability levels, including aspects of word structure and clitic usage \cite{habash2025guidelines}. In the present study, D3Tok is used as the morphological segmentation tool within the experimental pipeline; its role and implementation are described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

\subsection{Lexical Ambiguity and Syntactic Variation}

Lexical ambiguity is another challenge in Arabic text processing. In unvowelled text, the same written form may permit different readings or interpretations depending on its linguistic context. For instance, the fully vowelled forms \textit{عِلْم} (knowledge), \textit{عَلَم} (flag), and \textit{عَلَّمَ} (he taught) illustrate how vowel patterns can distinguish lexical or grammatical interpretations. In ordinary unvowelled writing, some of these distinctions may not be explicitly represented, making contextual information important for interpretation.

Arabic also exhibits variation in syntactic structure and word order. Understanding a sentence may require identifying grammatical relationships between words rather than relying solely on their positions in the sequence. Moreover, texts with similar lengths may differ in syntactic complexity, vocabulary, and the amount of contextual knowledge required for comprehension. These observations suggest that word counts and sentence length alone may not adequately characterize text difficulty.

Contextual language representations can help models capture relationships between words and their surrounding context. However, contextual representations do not automatically guarantee accurate readability predictions. The difficulty of a text depends on the interaction of its linguistic characteristics with the knowledge and proficiency of its intended readers. Consequently, the contribution of different linguistic features must be assessed empirically within the readability task.

\subsection{Data Availability and Fine-Grained Readability Levels}

The availability of annotated data is an important consideration in developing and evaluating Arabic readability assessment systems. The Balanced Arabic Readability Evaluation Corpus (BAREC) provides 69,441 Arabic sentences distributed across 19 ordered readability levels \cite{elmadani2025barec}. This resource enables the systematic evaluation of models on a fine-grained readability task.

Despite the availability of this benchmark, predicting 19 distinct ordered levels presents a demanding learning problem. A model must distinguish between neighboring levels while avoiding predictions that are substantially different from the reference labels. This requires attention not only to the linguistic representation of each sentence but also to the structure of the target labels.

Conventional multiclass classification treats readability levels as separate classes and, with standard cross-entropy loss, does not explicitly penalize errors according to the ordinal distance between labels. Ordinal learning methods offer alternative formulations that incorporate the ordering of the target levels into model training. Their potential relevance to Arabic readability assessment motivates the investigation conducted in this thesis. The theoretical foundations of these approaches and their relationship to previous work are reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review}.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Readability assessment can be formulated as a classification or regression task. However, when readability is represented by ordered levels, the target labels have an ordinal structure that should be considered during both model training and evaluation. In particular, the distance between a predicted level and its reference level provides information about the severity of a prediction error. For example, predicting level 10 for a sentence whose reference level is 11 represents a smaller ordinal discrepancy than predicting level 1.

Conventional multiclass classification with cross-entropy loss treats the target levels as distinct classes and does not explicitly incorporate their ordinal distances into the standard training objective. Regression and ordinal-learning methods offer alternative ways to model ordered targets, but their effectiveness may depend on the prediction head, the learning objective, the decision rule, and the experimental protocol. It is therefore necessary to compare these approaches under controlled conditions rather than assume that a particular formulation is inherently superior.

This issue is particularly relevant to fine-grained Arabic readability assessment, where the BAREC corpus provides 19 ordered readability levels. The task requires models to distinguish between neighboring levels while limiting predictions that are substantially distant from the reference annotations. Evaluating such models solely through exact-match accuracy may not fully characterize the ordinal severity of their errors.

Accordingly, this thesis investigates the following research question:

\begin{quote}
Under a fixed AraBERTv2 backbone, D3Tok morphological segmentation, and a consistent BAREC experimental protocol, how do selected classification, regression, and ordinal-learning approaches compare in terms of Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK), exact-match accuracy, and prediction-error distance?
\end{quote}

To address this question, the study compares prediction heads and loss functions while maintaining a consistent principal data pipeline and benchmark protocol. It also examines the relationship between exact-match performance and ordinal agreement, the distribution of prediction errors, and the behavior of selected methods under alternative evaluation settings.

Two complementary experimental extensions are considered: a two-phase training strategy combining mean squared error (MSE) with a differentiable Soft-QWK objective, and a computationally lighter setting in which the encoder is frozen and linear prediction heads are trained on cached representations. These extensions provide additional evidence about optimization behavior and computational requirements, while remaining distinct from the principal controlled comparison.

\section{General Readability Assessment}}

\subsection{Traditional Readability Formulas}}





Early readability assessment research relied mainly on handcrafted formulas designed to estimate text difficulty using measurable linguistic features. Common features include average sentence length, word length, syllable counts, and the proportion of complex words. These features are combined to produce a readability score or an estimated educational grade level, allowing texts to be assessed without training a machine learning model \cite{dubay2004principles}.

Several formulas became widely used in English readability assessment. The Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) formula estimates readability using sentence length and syllables per word, with higher scores indicating easier text. The Flesch--Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) uses related features but expresses the result as an estimated U.S. school grade. The Gunning Fog Index estimates the education level required to understand a text using average sentence length and the proportion of complex words. The SMOG (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook) formula estimates reading grade level primarily from the frequency of polysyllabic words \cite{flesch1948readability,kincaid1975derivation,gunning1952technique,smog}.

Table~\ref{tab:traditional_readability} summarizes these four widely known formulas and their principal features.

Despite their simplicity and low computational cost, traditional readability formulas have important limitations. They rely on a restricted set of surface-level features and do not directly represent contextual meaning, discourse coherence, or the full range of syntactic and semantic relationships in a text. Consequently, two texts with similar sentence lengths and word-level statistics may still differ substantially in meaning and comprehension difficulty.

Their direct application to Arabic also presents challenges. These formulas were designed around properties of English, including English syllable counts and word-complexity conventions. Arabic has different morphological and orthographic characteristics, so the same features cannot automatically be assumed to measure Arabic readability reliably \cite{habash2010introduction,nassiri2023approaches}.

These limitations motivated research into data-driven approaches. Rather than relying exclusively on fixed formulas, machine learning methods learn relationships between linguistic features and annotated readability levels from training data. The next subsection reviews these approaches.




\subsection{Machine Learning Approaches}

Machine learning approaches treat readability assessment as a prediction task in which a model learns the relationship between linguistic features and readability labels from annotated texts. Unlike traditional readability formulas, which combine features according to a predefined rule, these methods estimate the relationship from examples in a training dataset.

Early feature-based systems typically represented texts using manually extracted linguistic and statistical features. These could include word and sentence lengths, lexical frequency, vocabulary richness, part-of-speech distributions, and syntactic complexity. The extracted features were then provided to a machine learning algorithm to predict the readability level of a text. Support Vector Machines (SVMs), decision trees, and other conventional classifiers and regressors have been used for this type of task \cite{schwarm2005reading}.

The effectiveness of these approaches depends on the quality of the selected features, the size and representativeness of the annotated dataset, and the learning algorithm. Although feature-based models can capture more information than simple readability formulas, they may not fully represent contextual meaning or complex relationships between words across a text. Their performance can also depend on language-specific feature engineering, which makes adapting a system to a language with different linguistic characteristics a non-trivial task.

Feature-based machine learning has also been used as a baseline for Arabic readability assessment. In the BAREC benchmark, conventional models were evaluated using D3Tok-based text representations. The reported results include a Support Vector Machine baseline with a Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) score of 56.5\% and a Decision Tree baseline with a score of 52.5\% \cite{elmadani2025barec}. These baselines provide a point of comparison for the deep learning and transformer-based systems discussed later in this chapter.

A further design choice concerns how readability labels are represented during training. Some systems formulate readability prediction as classification, treating each level as a separate category, while others use regression to predict a numerical value. These formulations differ in how they represent the relationship between readability levels, particularly when the labels have a natural order. The next subsection introduces classification and regression as two common formulations of readability assessment.

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches}




Deep learning approaches extended readability assessment by learning representations directly from text, reducing the need for manually engineered linguistic features. Unlike conventional machine learning systems, which typically depend on features such as word frequency, sentence length, and syntactic complexity, neural networks can learn task-relevant patterns from training data.

Several neural architectures have been explored for readability prediction, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs), and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). RNNs process text as a sequence, while LSTMs use gating mechanisms to help preserve information over longer sequences. CNNs apply filters to text representations to learn local patterns that may be relevant to linguistic complexity. These architectures have been investigated in neural readability assessment research \cite{martinc2021supervised}.

The choice of architecture affects which textual patterns a model can learn. However, deep learning systems still depend on the quality and quantity of training data, and recurrent architectures process sequences step by step. These limitations helped motivate the adoption of transformer-based pretrained language models, which use self-attention to represent relationships between tokens across a sequence.

\subsection{Transformer-Based Language Models}

Transformer-based language models represent a major development in Natural Language Processing (NLP). The Transformer architecture uses self-attention to model relationships between tokens within a sequence, allowing the model to build contextual representations without processing tokens strictly one by one as recurrent networks do \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) uses a Transformer encoder to learn representations that incorporate context from both the left and right sides of a token. Its original pretraining procedure includes masked language modeling and next sentence prediction, followed by fine-tuning for downstream tasks \cite{devlin2019bert}.

Pretrained transformer models can be adapted to tasks such as text classification by fine-tuning their contextual representations on task-specific data. For readability assessment, this allows a model to use contextual linguistic information rather than relying exclusively on manually designed features. However, the model architecture alone does not determine how readability levels are predicted: the prediction head, training objective, and representation of the target labels also influence the learning process.

\subsection{AraBERTv2 for Arabic NLP}


AraBERTv2 is a pretrained Transformer language model developed specifically for Arabic Natural Language Processing (NLP) \cite{antoun2020arabert}. It is based on the BERT architecture and uses 12 layers, 12 attention heads, and hidden representations of 768 dimensions. The model was pretrained on a large Arabic text corpus.

An important consideration when using AraBERTv2 is its text preprocessing. AraBERT models have used different preprocessing strategies, including morphological segmentation with the Farasa segmenter \cite{darwish2016farasa}. Farasa separates clitics from their host words using a segmentation convention that marks boundaries with the ``+'' character. Since the input representation can affect how well a pretrained model processes Arabic text, the choice of segmentation is relevant to the present study, which uses D3Tok with AraBERTv2


The BAREC benchmark provides empirical support for selecting AraBERTv2 as the backbone in this study. Among the four Arabic pretrained models evaluated, AraBERTv2 achieved a higher development-set QWK with D3Tok input than with plain word input, increasing from 76.3\% to 80.0\%. The other evaluated models did not show the same improvement under D3Tok preprocessing \cite{elmadani2025barec}. These findings motivate the use of AraBERTv2 with D3Tok as the fixed backbone for the experiments in this thesis.

\subsection{Arabic Readability Datasets}

Building and evaluating automatic readability assessment systems requires annotated datasets and suitable evaluation resources. Arabic readability research has developed several such resources, although the availability of publicly accessible benchmark datasets remains a challenge \cite{nassiri2023approaches}.

Several Arabic readability datasets and related resources have appeared in recent years. The SAMER project produced a five-level readability lexicon for Standard Arabic covering 40,687 lemma and part-of-speech pairs \cite{alkhalil2020samer}. This resource later served as the basis for the SAMER corpus for Arabic text simplification \cite{alhafni2024samer}. DARES provided a readability assessment dataset collected from Saudi school materials \cite{elhaj2024dares}. The OSMAN metric adapted readability scoring to Arabic by accounting for script-specific markers and diacritization \cite{elhaj2016osman}. ReadMe++ introduced a multilingual readability benchmark covering several domains, including an Arabic subset relevant to Arabic readability research \cite{naous2024readme}. Liberato et al. explored strategies for Arabic readability modeling using pretrained language models on the SAMER corpus \cite{liberato2024strategies}.

These resources contribute to Arabic readability research, but they differ in their purpose, annotation schemes, and level of granularity. The Balanced Arabic Readability Evaluation Corpus (BAREC) is particularly relevant to this thesis because it supports fine-grained readability assessment using 19 ordered levels \cite{elmadani2025barec}. The next subsection describes BAREC's characteristics, annotation scheme, and data organization in more detail.


\subsection{The BAREC Dataset}

The Balanced Arabic Readability Evaluation Corpus (BAREC) was introduced as a large-scale benchmark dataset for Arabic readability assessment \cite{elmadani2025barec}. The corpus contains 69,441 Arabic sentences spanning more than one million words, collected from 1,922 documents across three domains (Arts \& Humanities, Social Sciences, and STEM) and three readership groups (Foundational, Advanced, and Specialized).

The main strength of BAREC is its fine-grained annotation scheme, which contains 19 ordered readability levels, ranging from kindergarten to postgraduate comprehension. These levels follow the Abjad order of the Arabic letters, from level 1 (\textit{alif}) to level 19 (\textit{qaf}). Through the BAREC pyramid, the 19 levels can also be mapped to coarser schemes of 7, 5, and 3 levels, allowing evaluation at different levels of granularity. Six expert Arabic educators manually annotated the corpus using an adaptation of the Taha-Thomure leveling guidelines; a separate paper describes the annotation protocol in detail \cite{habash2025guidelines}. The reported average pairwise inter-annotator agreement, measured using Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK), is 81.8\%. The dataset is divided at the document level into training (80\%), development (10\%), and test (10\%) sets to reduce the risk of data leakage.

\subsection{Ordinal Regression and Distance-Aware Learning}
Ordinal regression is a machine learning approach designed for prediction tasks in which the target labels have a natural order \cite{gutierrez2016ordinal}. Unlike standard classification, which treats the labels as separate categories, ordinal regression explicitly represents their ordering. This property makes ordinal methods relevant to readability assessment, where each text is assigned a level along an ordered scale.

For example, predicting readability level 10 when the correct level is 11 represents a smaller error in level distance than predicting level 1. However, not all methods that account for label ordering penalize errors in proportion to their distance. Some methods encode the order through their output structure, while others explicitly incorporate distance-sensitive penalties into the training objective.

Several approaches have been proposed to address these different aspects of ordinal prediction. CORAL and CORN incorporate ordinal information through cumulative prediction structures. CORAL learns a set of binary decisions associated with ordered thresholds, using a shared projection to encourage consistent ordinal predictions \cite{cao2020coral}. CORN also uses threshold-based binary tasks, but models them conditionally to construct cumulative probabilities \cite{shi2023deep}. Neither method directly optimizes QWK or explicitly weights every prediction error according to its numerical distance from the true level.

Other methods incorporate ordinal information through their loss functions. Ordinal Log Loss (OLL) weights the probability assigned to incorrect levels according to their distance from the correct level \cite{castagnos2022ordinal}. Soft-label methods replace the one-hot target with a distribution that assigns greater probability to levels closer to the correct one \cite{bertinetto2020soft}. Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) compares the cumulative distributions of predicted and target labels, thereby accounting for the ordering of the levels \cite{hou2017emd}.

Soft-QWK takes a different approach by optimizing a differentiable objective designed to approximate Quadratic Weighted Kappa \cite{delatorre2018wkl,diaz2019softlabels}. Because this objective depends on predictions across a batch, it can behave differently from a conventional per-example loss. The STBW system addresses this by using a mean squared error (MSE) warm-up before Soft-QWK optimization \cite{trigui2025stbw}. This training strategy is relevant to the experiments in this thesis.

\subsection{Pairwise Ranking Objectives for Readability Assessment}
\label{subsec:pairwise_ranking}

Pairwise ranking provides an alternative approach to readability assessment by learning relative difficulty from pairs of texts. Instead of predicting a readability category directly, a ranking model learns to order two texts according to their relative readability. Depending on the objective, the model may learn whether one text is more difficult than another without explicitly modeling the numerical distance between their readability levels.

Lee and Vajjala \cite{lee2022neural} proposed a neural pairwise ranking model for automatic readability assessment. Their approach uses a BERT-based model to compare two texts and applies Pairwise Logistic Loss to learn their relative reading difficulty. They evaluated the approach on three English datasets, one French dataset, and one Spanish dataset. In zero-shot cross-lingual experiments, a model trained on English data achieved ranking accuracy above 80\% on both French and Spanish, demonstrating the potential of pairwise ranking for cross-lingual readability assessment.

Trokhymovych et al. \cite{trokhymovych2024open} developed an open multilingual system for scoring the readability of Wikipedia articles. Their work introduced a dataset covering 14 languages, constructed by aligning Wikipedia articles with corresponding articles from simplified or children's encyclopedias. The proposed system adapts the neural pairwise ranking approach to produce an individual readability score at inference time. It uses a Margin Ranking Loss to encourage texts with higher reference readability levels to receive higher predicted scores. The authors report ranking accuracy above 80\% across the 14 languages in their zero-shot evaluation setting.

These studies demonstrate the potential of pairwise objectives for learning relative readability, but their experimental settings differ from the fine-grained Arabic sentence-level task considered in this thesis. The two papers also use distinct pairwise objectives: Lee and Vajjala employ Pairwise Logistic Loss, whereas Trokhymovych et al. use Margin Ranking Loss. These objectives should therefore not be treated as interchangeable.

In this thesis, two pairwise-ranking-inspired regression configurations, RANK and PLR, are evaluated on BAREC alongside other prediction heads and learning objectives. The configurations combine a mean squared error (MSE) term with, respectively, a margin-based ranking term and a pairwise logistic term. This design allows the experiments to examine whether incorporating pairwise ordering information improves readability prediction under the controlled experimental conditions described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. The exact objective formulations and their implementation are presented in that chapter.

Within the scope of the BAREC benchmark and Shared Task papers reviewed in this chapter, we did not identify an evaluation of these specific RANK and PLR configurations. This motivates their inclusion in the present comparison, without implying that pairwise ranking itself is new to readability assessment or that these objectives have never been applied to BAREC in any other work.

ZAI uses CORAL to represent the ordered target labels through cumulative ordinal outputs, whereas STBW combines scalar regression with an optimization stage based on Soft-QWK. Their blind-test results are close, but this numerical proximity does not establish that the methods are equivalent or that their prediction heads alone explain the observed performance. Instead, the principal experiments aim to isolate the behavior of selected prediction heads and learning objectives while keeping the principal encoder, input representation, data splits, and training protocol consistent. The exact configurations and the extent to which each experimental component is held fixed are detailed in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

Accordingly, the research gap is not that ordinal learning or alternative loss functions have never been applied to BAREC. Rather, the reviewed studies do not provide a directly controlled comparison of all the selected configurations under the same experimental conditions. This thesis addresses that gap by evaluating the selected classification, regression, ordinal, soft-label, and pairwise-ranking approaches within a consistent BAREC pipeline, using QWK as the principal metric and complementary measures to characterize exact-match performance and prediction-error distance.
